# Responsiveness

One of the seven Rapport Dimensions. Responsiveness measures how well you respond to the other person's statements and questions in a timely and relevant manner. High scores mean you are engaged and on-topic: you answer what was actually asked, quickly.

## Why it matters

Slow or scattered replies make people feel unheard. Answering what they actually asked, quickly, keeps them leaning in.

## The observable behavior

Respond to their last sentence first, before adding anything of your own.

## Transfer plan (from your coaching path)

- **Do**: answer the question they actually asked in your first sentence.
- **When**: whenever they finish a thought or ask you something directly.
- **Proof**: your Responsiveness score rises on your next analyzed call.

## How to read your score

Scored 0-100 like every dimension, shown on the Trust and Rapport radar. It gates belts at the shared per-belt bar (Yellow 50, Blue 65, Purple 78, Brown 85, Black 89; see [[belt-system]]). For overall-score bands, see [[score-bands]]. The exact blend of inputs is part of the scoring engine; the related measurements below show the behaviors that move it.

## Related measurements

- [[adjacency-pairs]]: question-answer and offer-accept pairs, and dispreferred (evasive or delayed) responses.

## Drills

- The two-second gift: after a big statement from the other side, hold a deliberate 1.5-2.0 second pause, then answer their point first. See [[drills-library]].
- One-sentence answer first: answer the asked question in one sentence before any context or story.

## FAQ

**Is faster always better?**
No. Habitually answering in under half a second reads as waiting to talk rather than listening. The product's guidance puts the comfortable response zone under about 2 seconds, with details and caveats in [[response-latency]].

**How is this different from Active Listening?**
[[active-listening]] is about proving you heard (acknowledgment, paraphrase). Responsiveness is about answering what was asked, relevantly and in good time. They usually rise together.
